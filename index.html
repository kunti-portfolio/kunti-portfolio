The Nurse I Am & The Nurse I Will Be

Why I chose nursing — and why it chose me

I chose nursing because, deep down, I have never been able to see myself doing anything else. From my early training in Kathmandu to my clinical placements across NSW, nursing has taught me that caring for people goes far beyond completing clinical tasks. It is about being present during some of the most vulnerable and uncertain moments of a person's life. That understanding continues to shape the way I communicate, think, and care as a future Registered Nurse.

I come from a place where healthcare is a privilege, not a right

Growing up in rural Nepal, I watched people in my community go without care they urgently needed — not because medicine could not help them, but because access to skilled and compassionate healthcare was limited. That reality never left me. It is why I pushed myself through a four-year nursing degree, sat the National Licensure Examination, and began my career on a neurosurgical ward as a newly Registered Nurse. Every patient I care for is someone's family, and I have never forgotten what it feels like to stand on the other side of that experience.

I believe clinical excellence and human compassion are inseparable

Some nurses are technically skilled. Some are warm and compassionate. The best nurses are both, at the same time, without compromise. During 18 months managing high-acuity neurosurgical patients in Nepal, I learned that a missed GCS change can cost a life — and that a frightened patient who does not trust their nurse may not share what they are experiencing. I trained myself to remain rigorous while being fully present. I listen before I assess. I explain before I act. Safe medication administration is a responsibility I approach with discipline, consistency, and a strong commitment to patient safety.

I want to serve the Australian community that welcomed me

Australia gave me the opportunity to rebuild my nursing career within a world-class healthcare system. I have spent three years in aged care, building a deeper understanding of how Australians age, how families grieve, and what dignity looks like in a residential setting at 2am. Northern NSW is a region with genuine healthcare need: an ageing population, rural access challenges, and diverse communities. I want to be part of that solution. I am not seeking a graduate program simply for a title — I am seeking the opportunity to give back to the community that has given me so much.

My goal is to grow into a specialist nurse who never stops learning

My immediate goal is to consolidate my skills as a safe and confident graduate nurse applicant within an acute NSW Health setting. My longer-term vision is to specialise in critical care and neuroscience nursing, shaped by my experience in Nepal's neurosurgical ICU and the satisfaction of recognising deterioration before it becomes crisis. I also hope to contribute to nursing education by supporting future graduates the way experienced nurses once supported me. Nursing is not a destination — it is a lifelong commitment to continuous growth.

What I believe makes me a good nurse
I notice what others miss

Whether it is a post-TURP patient with dropping catheter output, a withdrawn patient concealing pain, or a documentation discrepancy before a home visit — I am trained to look beyond the obvious. Clinical reasoning is a habit for me, not simply a response to crisis.

I speak up when patient safety depends on it

I raised a falls communication gap at Lismore Base Hospital. I identified a medication plan discrepancy before a palliative home visit. In Nepal, I led ward education after recognising a GCS documentation inconsistency. Speaking up respectfully and clearly is something I do because patient safety depends on it.

I bring two healthcare systems into one nursing practice

My experience in Nepal taught me how to work under resource constraints, manage high patient loads, and make independent clinical decisions under pressure. My experience in Australia has strengthened my understanding of documentation standards, multidisciplinary teamwork, and person-centred care. Together, these experiences have shaped a broader and more adaptable nursing perspective.

I care about people, not just patients

I remember names. I learn what matters to each resident at St Joseph's — their morning routine, the music they enjoy, and the family members they worry about. That is not extra care. That is nursing. A patient who feels genuinely seen experiences care very differently from someone who feels processed.
